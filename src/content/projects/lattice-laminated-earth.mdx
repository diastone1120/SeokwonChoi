## Context

Construction accounts for a large share of global carbon emissions, and conventional
materials like cement carry a heavy footprint. This project asks whether an ancient
material — earth — can be reintroduced through digital fabrication as a genuinely
low-carbon structural option.

<FigureCard src="/images/issue-stats.jpg" num="01" caption="Carbon emissions by industry sector, and the case for alternative materials" />

## Material system

A lattice core is 3D-printed using FGF (Fused Granular Fabrication) from bio-based
feedstocks, then laminated with an earth-and-lime mixture reinforced with natural
fibers.

<FigureCard src="/images/diagram-overall.jpg" num="02" caption="Element / material layer diagram" />

<FigureCard src="/images/diagram-composition.jpg" num="03" caption="Lattice core composition & mixture formulation" />

## Process

Seashell waste is calcined into quicklime, hydrated into slaked lime, then combined
with earth to form the laminating mixture. Multiple fiber types were tested —
coconut fiber, kenaf, rice husk, rice husk charcoal, agar, seaweed furcata, and wheat
straw — each evaluated for cost, environmental impact, and resource availability.

<FigureCard src="/images/workflow.jpg" num="04" caption="Overall research and fabrication workflow" />

## Two fabrication approaches

The lattice geometry was explored through two distinct methods — one built as a
single continuous structure, the other as combinable tiled units.

<div class="method-block">

### Structural method

Printed as a single curved form directly on a net-reinforced base layer using
non-planar toolpaths, then assembled from five units into a 1800mm arch —
accounting for joint vulnerability and gravity-induced deflection.

<FigureCard src="/images/structural-method.jpg" num="05" caption="Structural method: print path & assembly" />

<PhotoBlock src="/images/structural-buildup.jpg" caption="Built prototype — printing, assembly & hardening of the structural arch" />

</div>

<div class="method-block">

### Segmentation method

Designed as saddle-shaped, non-planar tiles with combinable curvature, modifiable
to different forms by changing the base-bed — allowing use as a discrete
architectural cladding or panel system rather than a single continuous structure.

<FigureCard src="/images/segmentation-method.jpg" num="06" caption="Segmentation method: tile unit & print path" />

<PhotoBlock src="/images/segmentation-buildup.jpg" caption="Tile unit printing, natural-fiber material samples, and finished results" />

</div>

## Outcomes

The work was published as a Master's thesis and presented at the 14th ISAIA 2024
Conference in Kyoto under the related paper, "Bio-Waste Wall: Advancing Sustainable
Construction with Bio-Based Materials and Manufacturing."

## Context

Advanced 3D printing and 3D scanning can meaningfully contribute to restoring
coastal ecosystems damaged by human activity and climate change. Surrounded by sea
on three sides, Korea's coastline has been degraded by landfill, coastal
construction, and eutrophication — leading to bleaching and the decline of rocky
ecosystems. Seagrass beds, among the highest-productivity zones in any marine
ecosystem, are a critical habitat and breeding ground worth protecting.

## Digital setup

Target tetrapod surfaces were captured via photogrammetry (Luma, Polycam) and
converted to mesh geometry in Rhino/Grasshopper. Toolpaths were generated and
simulated for an ABB IRB1200 six-axis robot fitted with a pellet extruder and
heated bed, printing a compound of seaweed waste, PBAT, and PLA into a
biodegradable bioplastic filament.

<FigureCard src="/images/digital-setup.jpg" num="02" tone="marine" caption="Digital setup — scan-to-print pipeline, robot configuration, and seaweed-bioplastic compound" />

## Three design methods

Three distinct approaches were tested for attaching a printed prosthesis to a
tetrapod's curved surface.

### Method 1 — Porosity design

Focused on maximizing porosity within the maximum allowable print thickness.
Kangaroo 2's sphere-collide simulation and a "stepslover" script were used to
generate the porous pattern, which was printed flat (planar) and then bent and
wrapped around the tetrapod surface by hand while still hot.

<FigureCard src="/images/method1.jpg" num="03" tone="marine" caption="Method 1 — porosity simulation and bending/wrapping fabrication" />

### Method 2 — Direct surface printing

Printed non-planar, directly onto the target surface using a simple net-like mesh
design, stacked a minimum of three layers to create voids between layers. Plane
alignment and tilting were adjusted along the toolpath to keep the print viable on
an irregular surface.

<FigureCard src="/images/method2.jpg" num="04" tone="marine" caption="Method 2 — direct non-planar printing with plane adjustment" />

### Method 3 — Layered shelter

Used the 'Anemone' looping technique in Grasshopper to slice stacked layers based
on offset values from the surface, with lower layers maximizing attachment area
before simplifying upward — creating an enclosed sanctuary for seagrass roots.

<FigureCard src="/images/method3.jpg" num="05" tone="marine" caption="Method 3 — offset-based layering to form a root sanctuary" />

## Conclusion

Each method suits a different habitat condition. Method 3 was judged the optimal
seagrass habitat against wave action, while Method 2 could be produced with the
least material. Methods 2 and 3, along with their fabrication logic, were
individually developed by Choi within the collaborative research.

<FigureCard src="/images/conclusion.jpg" num="06" tone="marine" caption="Comparison of the three methods across design, printing, and attachment" />

## In the field

A Method 3 prosthesis was installed on a tetrapod in Tongyeong, Korea, and observed
for seagrass growth and structural stability under real coastal conditions.
